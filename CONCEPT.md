# Signal & Noise — concept

**Signal & Noise** is a series of generative artworks where the algorithm *is* the security lesson. Each piece runs a classic emergent system — Voronoi tessellation, slime-mold agents, curl-noise flow, reaction-diffusion — and lets a defensive security idea fall out of its behavior instead of being drawn on top of it.

A network is territory, so segmentation is drawn as a tessellation: points relax under Lloyd's algorithm until the cells feel inevitable, and a breach floods only through the borders a firewall rule leaves open. The size of the red stain is the blast radius. Nothing about it is painted; it is counted, hop by hop, from an adjacency graph.

An attacker is a search process, so lateral movement is drawn as Physarum: thousands of agents sense, turn and deposit, and without any central plan their trails converge on the shortest routes from a phished workstation to the crown jewels. Hardened tiers act as dead ground where trails cannot persist, and the network visibly reroutes or starves.

Normal is a shape, so a traffic baseline is drawn as a divergence-free curl-noise field that thousands of particles obey. Anomalies are the few that refuse it — a straight, patient beacon cutting across the current — and they are flagged by measuring their disagreement with the field, the same way behavior analytics flags a host that stops looking like itself.

An outbreak is chemistry, so incident response is drawn with the Gray-Scott model: a self-replicating pattern spreads from patient zero, a containment boundary closes around it, eradication raises the kill rate inside, and the field returns to rest. Spread, contain, eradicate, recover — the lifecycle plays out as a phase change.

Every piece is seeded and reproducible: the same seed always gives the same composition, and every parameter is tuned slowly, by hand, until the system reads clearly at a glance and still rewards a long look. The goal is work that looks like it took hundreds of iterations, because it should — and a viewer who knows security should feel the concept before they read a single label.
